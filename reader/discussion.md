# Which deployment promises can a model support?

Imagine a system that consistently accepts a short delay to give a more accurate answer. One reading is that training has produced a stable preference for accuracy. A second is that such a preference usefully summarizes the choices we observed. A third uses coherent choice as a standard against which to judge the system. These are the literal, behavioral and normative interpretations of preferences considered by Bergemann, Koh and Morris. Each can motivate studying incentives. What the human can infer from a choice record depends on which actions were feasible, which information the agent had, and whether its incentives persist in the deployment under consideration. [Original paper, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.01595v1#S6) · [Test a choice record](12-capstone.html#exercise-1)

## Three ways the question changes

An entire sequence of actions can be treated as one choice of trajectory when the parties commit in advance. If the human can revise tomorrow's payment after seeing today's action, the agent must forecast that revision. A promised payment can then fail precisely on the history where it is due. [Work through a commitment example](12-capstone.html#exercise-4)

Evaluation awareness raises an information question. When the same apparent request can come from an evaluator or a user, the system's posterior probability of being tested can change its preferred action. Designing which features of the setting it observes changes that posterior. A separate question is robustness to uncertainty about the agent's capabilities and information. A mechanism justified under one conditional distribution of a peer's report can lose its incentive margin under another. The paper identifies these issues as directions for further work. ## Where the appendix enters the argument

Appendix A supplies two mathematical steps behind the single-agent applications. Lemma 2 explains why a constrained least-squares fit also minimizes the transformed loss used in the sandbagging problem. The proof groups capability values on a finite partition, compares the optimality conditions, and then passes to a monotone continuum solution. Proposition 4 starts with arbitrary closed permission sets for different bias reports. A distance inequality compares each set to a cap, and the order of those comparison caps allows one common cap to serve all types. The one-type inequality also underlies permission delegation in Example V. The proofs of the peer, competition and oversight results appear alongside their statements in the main text.
